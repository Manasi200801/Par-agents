A full explanation of the data, the problem, the approach, and every decision made.
Written 2026-06-28.

================================================================================
CHAPTER 1: THE COMPANY AND THE PROBLEM
================================================================================

Alpine Manufacturing GmbH is a fictional industrial equipment company operating
across Europe. They make physical goods — bearings, precision couplings,
industrial resins, drill components — the kind of hardware that ends up inside
other machines. Their products are sold through 18 regional sales organisations
spread across the EU: DACH (Germany, Austria, Switzerland), BeNeLux, Western EU,
and so on. Within each sales organisation, they sell through multiple channels —
CH01, CH02, CH03, CH04 — which represent different customer types or distribution
routes (key accounts, distributors, wholesale, and so on).

Every month, Alpine needs to answer a very specific question for each product in
each region: how many units should we commit to produce and ship next month? This
is called the monthly demand commit, and it is the central decision of the entire
supply chain.

If you commit too many, you overproduce. Excess stock sits in warehouses tying up
capital and eventually becomes obsolescence risk. If you commit too few, you can't
fulfil customer orders. Big customers get angry. Revenue disappears. Either mistake
costs money.

Alpine has a statistical forecasting system — a machine learning model — that looks
at historical sales patterns and outputs a predicted demand number for each
product-organisation-channel combination every month. This number is called the
machine forecast. It is the system's best guess based purely on the numbers.

But here's the practical problem: the machine doesn't know about the phone call the
sales rep just had with a key customer. It doesn't know that a competitor is
rumoured to be exiting the market. It doesn't know that the product just went on
promotion. It doesn't know there's a stock shortage coming from a supplier. These
things live in the heads of the demand planners — the human professionals whose job
is to review the machine forecast and either accept it or adjust it.

When a planner adjusts the forecast number, that adjusted number is called an
override. The planner writes down a reason — "I heard this competitor is shutting
down their DACH operations" — and submits their number. The organisation then plans
production and logistics around that committed number.

The question that kicked off this entire project was: are the human overrides
actually helping? Are planners making things better or worse when they deviate from
the machine? And if both happen, when does each happen?

CHAPTER 2: THE DATA
================================================================================

Before writing a single line of code, we had to understand exactly what data Alpine
has. The dataset is stored as a collection of parquet files — a compressed,
column-oriented file format that is fast to query on a laptop without needing a
running database server.

There are 19 source data tables, plus additional forecast pipeline output files.
Here is what each one contains and why it matters:

alpine_sales_actuals.parquet
  The foundation. Contains approximately 2.8 million rows of actual monthly sales
  transactions across all 600 products, 18 sales organisations, and 4 channels.
  Each row has: date, product, sales org, channel, quantity sold (in business
  units), and revenue in euros. This is the ground truth — what actually shipped,
  what actually sold. When we want to know what a product has been selling recently,
  we read this file.

alpine_statistical_forecast.parquet
  The machine-generated demand forecast. Gives the model's prediction for each
  product-org-channel combination for future months. The "system forecast" number
  in the UI comes ultimately from this source.

alpine_demand_plan.parquet
  The historical planner-adjusted demand plan — what planners committed to each
  month in the past. Distinct from the statistical forecast; it's the human-approved
  number that the supply chain actually executed against.

alpine_products.parquet
  A catalogue of all 600 SKUs (Stock Keeping Units — individual product codes).
  Each product has: product ID, product name, product group, category (e.g.,
  Bearings, Couplings, Industrial Resins), and business unit. This is how we know
  that CP-0271 is "Premium Drills Mark II 1" sitting in the Precision Components
  business unit.

alpine_sales_org.parquet
  The 18 sales organisation definitions. Each org has an ID (SO04, SO08, etc.) and
  a region group (DACH, BeNeLux, Western EU, etc.).

alpine_channels.parquet
  The 4 channel definitions (CH01-CH04). Channels segment how products reach
  customers.

alpine_customers.parquet
  Customer-level data including whether a customer is currently flagged as
  "declining." Used by the Demand agent to spot weakening demand at the account
  level, which a purely aggregate forecast wouldn't show.

alpine_suppliers.parquet
  Supplier reliability profiles. Which suppliers serve which products, their
  historical OTIF (On Time In Full delivery rate), and their risk level.

alpine_stock_on_hand.parquet
  A series of inventory snapshots: for each product and sales org, how many units
  were sitting in stock on each snapshot date, and what their combined value was in
  euros. This tells the Supply agent whether there's a buffer if demand surprises,
  or whether we're already dangerously lean.

alpine_future_order_book.parquet
  Confirmed customer orders that have already been placed but not yet fulfilled.
  These are real demand signals — a customer has already committed to buying. If the
  order book total significantly exceeds the machine forecast, that's evidence the
  forecast is too low.

alpine_production_data.parquet
  Monthly production records: what was planned to be produced vs what actually came
  off the production line. The "attainment rate" (actual divided by planned) tells
  the Supply agent whether raising a commit is realistic — if production has been
  running at 85% of plan for three months, promising more won't help.

alpine_marketing_spends.parquet
  Marketing campaign activity: which campaigns ran for which product categories in
  which sales orgs, when, what type (promotion, brand campaign, trade event), and
  how much was spent. The Commercial agent uses this to spot whether an active
  campaign might boost demand beyond what the forecast captured.

alpine_business_plan.parquet
  The annual financial plan: how much revenue and gross margin each business unit
  is expected to generate per sales org per month. The Finance agent checks whether
  a planner's override creates a significant deviation from the financial plan — a
  big downward override in a region that's already behind plan is a different
  situation from one in a region that's running ahead.

alpine_price_changes.parquet
  The history of price changes for each product-org-channel: when the list price
  changed and to what. The Finance and Commercial agents both use this. A recent
  price increase can explain a volume decline in the machine forecast; a price cut
  might signal incoming demand uplift.

alpine_purchase_orders.parquet
  Purchase orders issued to suppliers: expected delivery week, actual delivery
  week, quantities. This is how the Supply agent computes the OTIF rate — if 40%
  of orders from a supplier arrived late, raising the commit on a product that
  depends on that supplier is risky.

alpine_delivery_data.parquet
  Outbound delivery performance records — what was shipped to customers and when.

alpine_returns.parquet
  Product return events: how many units were returned, why, and when. The Demand
  agent checks returns because they signal quality issues, product obsolescence, or
  overshipment — any of which would support a downward override.

alpine_cost_breakdown.parquet
  Product-level cost structure: manufacturing cost, logistics cost, overhead
  allocation. Used by the Finance agent for margin calculations.

alpine_tariff_reference.parquet
  Import tariff rates by product category and sourcing region over time. If tariffs
  on a product category have increased, the cost of goods rises, which affects
  margin and can influence pricing decisions. The Finance agent surfaces this.

alpine_actuals_plan_forecast_monthly.parquet
  A pre-aggregated monthly mart that brings together actuals, demand plan, and
  statistical forecast in one flat table. Used for the FVA replay analysis.

Forecast pipeline output files:

live_forecasts.parquet
  The actual machine forecast numbers at the granular item level for recent months
  (April-June 2026 in the current dataset). This is what Compass reads to show the
  "system forecast" number on screen.

meta_data.parquet
  A mapping from item_id (the forecast system's internal ID) to the business
  identifiers: product, sales org, channel, category, business unit. This bridge
  table is how we translate between the forecasting system's world and the
  commercial world.

final_backtest_forecasts.parquet, final_error_metrics.parquet,
final_overall_error_metrics.parquet
  Backtest outputs from the forecasting model: what would the model have predicted
  at each historical cutoff date, and how far off was it? These power the FVA
  replay analysis.

In total, the dataset covers 600 products x 18 sales orgs x 4 channels = up to
43,200 possible item combinations, though not all products are sold in all regions.
The hierarchy table in Compass's database contains only the combinations that
actually have live forecasts.

CHAPTER 3: THE FIRST QUESTION — DO OVERRIDES HELP?
================================================================================

Before building anything, we had to understand the baseline. We ran a Forecast
Value Added (FVA) analysis on the historical data.

FVA is simple in concept. For each month, you have three numbers: the machine
forecast, the planner's committed number, and the actual sales that happened. You
compute two errors:
  Machine error  = |actual - machine forecast|
  Planner error  = |actual - planner commit|

If machine error > planner error, the planner's override helped (they got closer to
reality). If machine error < planner error, the override hurt (the machine was
closer). FVA = machine error - planner error, so positive FVA means the human added
value.

We ran this analysis across 23 planning cycles (roughly two years of monthly
decisions), covering 68,126 item-month observations and 2.8M underlying sales rows.

The results were striking:

Overall, planners improved accuracy by a large margin.
  Machine MAE:  2,013 units per item-month
  Planner MAE:  1,574 units per item-month
  Improvement:  21.8% reduction in absolute error
  Machine WAPE: 19.2%
  Planner WAPE: 15.0%

But the improvement was not uniform.
  Upward overrides (planner commits MORE than machine): helped in 71% of cases
  Downward overrides (planner commits LESS than machine): hurt in 66% of cases

Most overrides were meaningful.
  75.7% of override events represented a change of more than 5% from the machine
  forecast. The median meaningful override was 13.9% away from machine value.
  45% of meaningful overrides were downward.

What this tells us: human planners have genuine insight that improves forecast
accuracy overall — but they have a systematic bias toward downward overrides that
frequently backfire. This creates the core use case for Compass: help planners make
better-calibrated decisions by confronting them with evidence before they commit,
and track performance over time so the pattern becomes visible.


================================================================================
CHAPTER 4: THE SOLUTION — COMPASS
================================================================================

Compass is an AI-assisted decision support tool for demand planners. The key word
is "support" — the human always makes the final call. Compass's job is to make
sure the planner has seen every relevant signal before they commit to a number.

The central workflow has five steps:

1. The planner selects a product, sales org, and channel.
2. They see the machine forecast alongside real historical sales data (recent
   run-rate, same month last year, stock on hand, value per unit).
3. They propose a number — their override — and write down why they want to change.
4. Compass runs a panel of specialist AI agents, each examining the decision from
   a different angle.
5. An AI reconciler synthesises all the evidence into a suggestion, the planner
   reviews it, and commits a final number.

Everything that happens in steps 4 and 5 is recorded in a database. Once actuals
arrive next month, every decision gets scored — did the final committed number
improve on the machine forecast? This creates a learning loop: every decision adds
to the track record, and the next decision benefits from that history.


================================================================================
CHAPTER 5: THE ARCHITECTURE
================================================================================

The technical stack was chosen for simplicity and speed. There are three layers:

THE DATA LAYER
  Raw parquet files from the Alpine dataset, read directly by Python via PyArrow.
  There is no data warehouse, no ETL pipeline. The parquet files are the source of
  truth, read on demand and cached in memory using Python's @lru_cache. This works
  because the dataset fits comfortably in RAM on a modern laptop and the parquet
  format is fast for columnar reads.

  One important technical issue: Alpine's data was exported with dictionary-encoded
  columns using uint32 indices — a compression technique where strings are stored as
  integer codes. Standard pd.read_parquet() doesn't handle these correctly and
  fails. Every loader goes through a custom PyArrow decoder
  (_decode_dictionary_columns) that casts dictionary columns back to their
  underlying string type before converting to a pandas DataFrame.

THE DATABASE LAYER
  A single DuckDB file (compass.duckdb) that stores everything Compass writes: the
  product-org-channel hierarchy, every committed decision, every event, and computed
  FVA scores. DuckDB is an in-process SQL engine — it runs embedded inside the
  Python application, needs no server, and the entire database is one file.

  Why DuckDB instead of PostgreSQL or SQLite?
  - PostgreSQL requires a running server — overkill for a demo.
  - SQLite is simpler but lacks columnar storage and analytical query performance.
  - DuckDB is the sweet spot: SQL interface, no server, analytical speed, small
    footprint.
  - Neo4j (graph database) was ruled out entirely — no graph-shaped problem here.

THE APPLICATION LAYER
  A Streamlit web app (app/main.py). Streamlit is a Python-first UI framework that
  turns scripts into interactive web pages. It handles state management, reactive
  re-rendering, and the HTTP server — all in Python. For a data product prototype,
  it removes weeks of frontend work.

CHAPTER 6: THE FIVE AGENTS — WHY FIVE AND WHAT EACH ONE DOES
================================================================================

When a planner proposes an override, the fundamental question is: what evidence
exists in the data that supports or challenges this decision? That evidence comes
from fundamentally different domains — demand signals, supply constraints, financial
exposure, market conditions, and historical performance. No single analysis captures
all of them.

We chose five agents because each one reads a different slice of the data and asks
a different question:

------------------------------------------------------------
AGENT 1: DEMAND (THREE SUB-SIGNALS)
------------------------------------------------------------

The Demand agent reads the future order book, customer data, and returns data.
It emits up to three signals:

Orders sub-signal:
  Looks at confirmed customer orders for this product in this sales org. If
  customers have already placed firm orders for 1,100 units and the machine
  forecast says 833, that's hard evidence the machine is too low — those orders
  already exist. Conversely, if the order book is light, that supports a downward
  override. Direction is set mechanically: if confirmed orders exceed the machine
  forecast, the signal contradicts a downward override.

Customers sub-signal:
  Flags declining accounts in this sales org — customers whose purchasing trend has
  been weakening. This is a signal that the aggregate sales picture is hiding a
  structural deterioration in the customer base that the historical patterns don't
  yet capture.

Returns sub-signal:
  Counts recent return events for this specific SKU. High returns signal quality
  problems or overshipment — evidence that the product is landing at customers who
  didn't really need it. This supports a downward view.

Why three sub-signals instead of one?
  Because the evidence types are genuinely different. Order book evidence is hard
  (confirmed money on the table). Customer trend evidence is directional (accounts
  changing behaviour). Returns evidence is a lagging indicator of product-market fit
  issues. Showing them as three separate cards lets the planner evaluate their
  quality independently rather than averaging them into one number they can't
  interrogate.

------------------------------------------------------------
AGENT 2: SUPPLY AND OPERATIONS
------------------------------------------------------------

The Supply agent reads stock on hand, purchase orders, and production data.
It asks: can we actually fulfil whatever number we commit to?

Stock cover signal:
  Takes the current stock quantity and divides it by the weekly demand rate
  (machine forecast / 4). If the result is less than 3 weeks of cover, a downward
  override could leave almost no buffer — if actual demand runs higher than the
  commit, a stockout happens fast.

Supplier OTIF signal:
  Looks at historical purchase orders for this product and computes what fraction
  arrived on time (on-time in full). If OTIF is below 80%, raising the forecast
  risks committing to a number that the supply chain can't reliably deliver.

Production attainment signal:
  Checks whether production has been hitting its targets. If a factory has been
  running at 85% of planned output for three months, the capacity to absorb a
  higher commit simply may not exist.

The supply agent always emits at least one signal even if all three thresholds
pass — it will report "stock cover is X weeks, no supply constraint identified."
This ensures the planner's panel is never silent. An empty panel is confusing;
a neutral "all clear" is informative.

------------------------------------------------------------
AGENT 3: FINANCE AND MARGIN
------------------------------------------------------------

The Finance agent reads the business plan, cost breakdown, tariff reference, and
price changes. It asks: what is the financial exposure of this override?

Revenue delta signal:
  Computes the revenue implication of the proposed override. If the planner
  proposes 500 units instead of 833, and the unit price is €78, that's a €26,000
  revenue reduction. It then compares this to the monthly business plan revenue for
  this business unit in this sales org. If the revenue delta exceeds 5% of the
  plan, the signal fires.

Tariff signal:
  Tracks whether import tariff rates on this product category have risen by more
  than 10% relative to the previous assessment. Rising tariffs increase landed cost,
  which can squeeze margins or force price adjustments.

Why Finance as a separate agent?
  A planner thinking about units doesn't automatically translate their decision into
  revenue. Making the financial stakes explicit — "this override reduces estimated
  revenue by €26k vs. the €320k monthly plan" — changes how a planner frames their
  decision.

------------------------------------------------------------
AGENT 4: COMMERCIAL AND MARKET
------------------------------------------------------------

The Commercial agent reads marketing spend records and price change history.
It asks: what commercial dynamics are active right now that might shift demand
away from history?

Campaign signal:
  If there are active marketing campaigns for this product category in this sales
  org, they may lift demand beyond what the statistical model captured. A trade
  fair, a promotional campaign, a key-account push — these are signals the machine
  didn't know about when it generated its forecast.

Price change signal:
  If the list price for this product has changed by more than 2% recently, it fires
  a signal. A price increase often dampens demand. A price cut often stimulates it.
  The machine's historical patterns were trained on prices that no longer apply.

Why separate from Finance?
  The Finance agent thinks in margin impact and plan deviation. The Commercial agent
  thinks in market dynamics — what forces in the external world are acting on this
  product right now. They ask different questions and produce different evidence.

------------------------------------------------------------
AGENT 5: MEMORY
------------------------------------------------------------

This agent is special because it doesn't read Alpine source data. It reads the
Compass database itself — the record of past decisions.

The Memory agent pulls two things:
1. The track record of this specific planner: how many decisions have they made,
   what fraction helped, what was their average FVA.
2. The track record of this reason class: when planners cited "competitor exit" in
   the past, how big were the overrides, how big was the actual impact, what
   fraction helped.

It then produces a calibrated suggestion — a number that the historical track
record for this reason type would imply. If past "competitor exit" overrides
averaged +20% but actual realised impacts averaged +13%, the memory agent nudges
the suggestion toward the more modest number.

Why does this matter?
  Because planners tend to overreact. The upward override bias found in the FVA
  analysis follows patterns by reason class. "Competitor exit" consistently produced
  overrides that overshot the actual demand impact. "Promotion" events produced
  overrides that often undershot. The memory agent makes this pattern explicit and
  personal.

Note: there is no memory.py agent file. Memory lookups are simple SQL queries,
not AI reasoning, so the memory context is assembled in the pipeline directly and
passed to the reconciler as structured data.


================================================================================
CHAPTER 7: THE RECONCILER — THE SINGLE SYNTHESIS CALL
================================================================================

Once all five agents have run, Compass has a collection of signals — directional
evidence with confidence scores and supporting data rows. The reconciler's job is
to synthesise all of this into a single recommendation.

The reconciler is built on Claude Opus 4.8, currently the most capable model
Anthropic makes. The choice matters because synthesis is a harder task than
pattern-matching. The reconciler has to:
  - Read evidence from five different domains
  - Understand that some signals contradict each other
  - Weight signals by their confidence and relevance
  - Produce a number that balances the evidence
  - Write 2-4 plain sentences explaining why

The prompt is structured in four sections:
  1. Decision context (product, machine value, planner's proposed value, reason
     text, classified reason class)
  2. Signals from all agents
  3. Memory and track record summary
  4. Task instructions and output format (JSON only)

One architectural constraint is enforced by a code comment at the top of the
reconciler file: this is the only place Opus is called. Never add a second call.

This is a cost discipline decision. Opus is the most expensive Claude model. Every
planner decision costs one Opus call. If Opus were called inside individual agents
or for follow-up analysis, the per-decision cost would multiply. The agents run on
rule-based Python (no model calls at all); only the synthesis step uses AI.

If no API key is configured, the reconciler falls back to a deterministic stub — a
hardcoded demo response. This means the application works completely without an API
key, which is essential for a demo that might be shown without internet access or
before billing is set up.


================================================================================
CHAPTER 8: HOW THE DATA FLOWS — ONE DECISION, START TO FINISH
================================================================================

Let's trace exactly what happens when a planner opens Compass and commits a
decision for CP-0271 in SO04 / CH01:

Step 1 — Load the context.
  Compass reads product metadata from alpine_products.parquet and sales org
  metadata from alpine_sales_org.parquet. These are cached in session state after
  the first load.

Step 2 — Get the machine forecast.
  _get_machine_value() queries live_forecasts.parquet via forecast_metadata.parquet.
  It tries next month, then current month, then one month back, then two months
  back — because the live forecast file only covers April-June 2026. It returns
  both the numeric value AND the specific month it resolved to (e.g., "2026-06-01").
  This month is then used everywhere: the planning period label, the "same month
  last year" lookup, and the story headline.

Step 3 — Compute the decision facts.
  compass/insights.py runs four computations from alpine_sales_actuals.parquet:

  Recent run-rate: the trailing 3-month average of actual monthly sales, with a
  partial-month trim. If the most recent month looks like a data cutoff (less than
  45% of the median of prior months), it's dropped to avoid misleading the average.

  Same month last year: the actual sales quantity for June 2025 (the calendar month
  one year before June 2026), looked up directly from the monthly actuals index.

  Value per unit: the revenue per unit over the trailing 6 months of actuals (total
  revenue divided by total quantity), reflecting realised price rather than list
  price.

  Stock position: the most recent stock-on-hand snapshot.

  All these functions are wrapped in @lru_cache — the first call reads the parquet
  file, subsequent calls return the cached result instantly.

  For CP-0271 / SO04 / CH01: run-rate ~1,241/mo, June 2025 ~1,605, value/unit ~€78,
  stock ~758 units.

Step 4 — The planner sees the story.
  The UI builds a headline sentence: "The system forecasts 833 units for Jun 2026
  — about 33% below the recent run-rate of ~1,241/mo and 48% below last year's
  1,605. At ~€78/unit that is roughly €65k in sales value."
  This sentence is generated from the real computed facts, not hardcoded.

Step 5 — The planner proposes a number and writes their reason.
  They move the slider to, say, 1,100, write "I heard our main competitor is exiting
  DACH. Expect demand to shift to us." Compass classifies the reason text using
  keyword matching — this becomes "competitor_exit" as the reason class.

Step 6 — The agent pipeline runs.
  Each of the five agents is called with a DecisionContext object containing the
  product, sales org, channel, machine value (833), override value (1,100), reason
  text, and reason class. Each agent returns a list of Signal objects. Each signal
  has: agent name, claim text, direction (supports/contradicts/neutral), magnitude
  (0-1), confidence (0-1), source table name, and evidence rows.

Step 7 — The reconciler synthesises.
  All signals plus the memory context are formatted into a prompt and sent to Opus
  4.8. Opus reads: five or more signals, the historical pattern for
  "competitor_exit" overrides, the planner's personal track record. It responds
  with JSON: a recommended value, a confidence level, and a rationale paragraph.

Step 8 — The planner reviews the evidence.
  The UI renders each signal as a card. Each card shows which way the evidence
  points (Suggests higher demand / Suggests lower demand / Broadly neutral) and the
  actual data rows the agent used. The planner can expand any card to see the raw
  evidence table.

Step 9 — The planner commits.
  They choose: accept the suggestion, keep their own number, or type something
  different. They click "Confirm and save."

Step 10 — The decision is written to DuckDB.
  write_decision() records everything: machine value, override value, reconciler
  value, final committed value, reason text, reason class, planner ID, timestamp.
  The FVA fields are left null until actuals arrive.

Step 11 — When actuals come in.
  score_decision(decision_id, actual) is called. It computes four errors (machine,
  proposed, reconciler, final), sets FVA = machine_error - final_error, and updates
  the decision record. Now this decision feeds into the track record views for the
  next planner who uses this reason class.


================================================================================
CHAPTER 9: THE SCORED MEMORY — THE LEARNING LOOP
================================================================================

The memory system is what separates Compass from a one-shot analysis tool. Every
committed decision is a data point. Every scored decision teaches the system
something about human override patterns.

In the DuckDB database, two SQL views are maintained automatically:

track_record_by_reason
  For each reason class (competitor_exit, promotion, supply_constraint, etc.):
  how many past decisions used this reason? What fraction improved accuracy? What
  was the average override percentage? What was the average realised impact
  percentage? This view updates live as decisions are scored.

track_record_by_planner
  For each individual planner (identified by name or ID): how many decisions have
  they made, what fraction were helpful, what was their average FVA?

When the Memory agent runs for a new decision, it queries both views. If a planner
with 14 past decisions has a 64% helpfulness rate and average FVA of +3.4, that's
a signal the reconciler factors in. If the reason class is "competitor_exit" and
past instances show planners consistently over-estimated the demand uplift
(overriding by +20% when actual impact was +13%), the reconciler knows to discount
the magnitude somewhat.

The calibrated suggestion — "based on similar past cases, a number near 960 has
worked well" — comes from applying the reason class's average realised impact to
the machine forecast. It's not a black-box model; it's a direct lookup of
historical performance.

CHAPTER 10: WHAT THE PLANNER SEES — THE UI
================================================================================

The Streamlit app has two views: Live Decision (the main workflow) and Replay
Dashboard (FVA analysis over historical cycles).

The Live Decision view is structured as four numbered steps:

STEP 1 — Pick what you're planning.
  Four dropdowns: product (600 options), sales org (18 options), channel (4
  options), and their name or ID. Changing the product resets the slider to the
  machine value so the planner always starts from a neutral position.

STEP 2 — Understand the forecast and set your number.
  Two columns. Left: the "The starting number" card. Shows the machine forecast as
  a large number, plus four mini-cards: recent monthly sales, same month last year,
  sales value per unit, and units in stock. Each mini-card has a note: "Forecast is
  33% below this" so the planner immediately sees the gap. Right: "Your decision"
  — slider and number input for their proposed override, a consequence preview
  ("Committing 267 units below the forecast. If demand lands near the forecast,
  about 267 units (~€21k) of demand could go unmet"), and the reason textarea.
  Above all of this sits the "What you're looking at" banner — a one-sentence story
  built from the computed facts.

STEP 3 — See what the checks found.
  After clicking "Check my decision", the five agent cards appear. A lead line
  summarises the lean: "4 of 5 checks point to stronger demand than the forecast."
  Each card shows the agent's finding, a directional chip (up/down/steady), the
  confidence level, and a link to expand and see the raw data rows. The suggested
  number appears below with the reasoning paragraph from Opus.

STEP 4 — Confirm and commit.
  Three radio options: use the suggested number, keep your own number, or type a
  different one. The final committed number is shown large. Clicking "Confirm and
  save" writes the decision to DuckDB and shows the Decision Record — a compact
  card recording all four numbers (forecast, your number, suggested, final), the
  reason, the planner's name, and a note that the system will score this once real
  sales arrive.

The Replay Dashboard reads two pre-computed cache files: headline_stats.json (the
aggregate numbers: MAE improved from 2,013 to 1,574, WAPE improved from 19.2% to
15.0%, 64.3% of meaningful overrides helped) and replay_chart_cache.parquet (the
cycle-by-cycle accuracy data for the chart). These files are generated once by the
setup script and served statically — computing FVA across 2.8M sales rows in real
time during a demo would be too slow.


================================================================================
CHAPTER 11: THE DESIGN DECISIONS THAT WEREN'T OBVIOUS
================================================================================

Why the slider starts at the machine value.
  When the planner opens the page, the slider sits exactly at the machine forecast.
  This forces the planner to consciously decide to move away from the machine. If
  the slider defaulted to last month's number or zero, planners would anchor to the
  wrong baseline. Starting at machine value encodes the question: "given what you
  know, how much do you want to deviate from this?"

Why the slider is unconstrained in the number input.
  The slider range is 50-175% of the machine value — a visual guide. But the number
  input box accepts any value. A planner who needs to type 2,000 when the machine
  says 400 can do so. The slider thumb clamps to the range boundary visually, but
  the actual override value comes from the number input and is never clamped. This
  prevents the slider's visual range from becoming an invisible policy constraint.

Why the consequence box exists.
  Before Compass, planners committed numbers without seeing the revenue-denominated
  stake. "267 units below the forecast" is abstract. "~€21k of demand could go
  unmet" is concrete. The consequence box computes this from the real unit price in
  the data and renders it every time the slider moves. It is the single most
  effective piece of UI at making the financial gravity of the decision visceral.

Why insights.py exists as a separate module.
  All the real-data computations (run-rate, last year, value per unit, stock) live
  in compass/insights.py, not in the view file. This separation means the view file
  stays readable and the insight computations are independently testable. The
  @lru_cache decorator means each computation runs once per product-org-channel
  combination per session, no matter how many times the UI re-renders.

Why there is no memory.py agent file.
  The memory context is assembled in the pipeline, which queries store.py for the
  track records. The memory signal card shown to the planner is a product of the
  reconciler including memory context in its prompt. Memory lookups are simple SQL
  queries, not AI reasoning, so they don't need an "agent" wrapper.

Why insights.py drops trailing partial months.
  The Alpine data has a cutoff date — the dataset was exported on a specific day.
  The most recent month in the actuals file is likely incomplete. If we averaged in
  a partial month, the run-rate would be artificially suppressed. The
  _complete_months() function checks: if the last month's quantity is less than 45%
  of the median of the preceding months, it looks like a cutoff artifact and gets
  dropped. The threshold is 45% rather than 50% because genuinely low months (a
  product that sold half its normal amount due to a one-off event) should not be
  dropped; only months that look truncated should be.

Why the month-alignment fix was needed.
  The planning period label originally showed "Jul 2026" (next month), but the
  live_forecasts.parquet only covers up to June 2026. The _get_machine_value()
  function resolves the actual model value from June 2026 via an offset loop
  [+1, 0, -1, -2]. Before the fix, "same month last year" used the label's month
  (July 2025 ~ 1,051 units) instead of the resolved month (June 2025 ~ 1,605
  units). The fix: _get_machine_value() now returns a tuple (value, resolved_month)
  and the resolved month is used everywhere — label, cycle_label, and the
  target_month argument to _decision_facts(). Result: CP-0271 now shows Jun 2026
  and "48% below last year" (1,605) instead of "20% below last year" (1,051).


================================================================================
THE COMPLETE PICTURE
================================================================================

Start to finish, here is what Compass is and why every piece exists:

THE PROBLEM
  Demand planners override machine forecasts every month, and nobody knows whether
  those overrides are helping or hurting.

THE FINDING
  They mostly help (21.8% MAE improvement overall) — but downward overrides hurt
  66% of the time, and the pattern is predictable by reason type.

THE SOLUTION
  Put the evidence in front of the planner before they commit, track every decision,
  and feed the track record back into the next decision.

THE DATA
  19 source tables covering sales history, inventory, suppliers, customers, orders,
  production, finance, marketing, pricing — all pre-existing Alpine data.

THE AGENTS
  Five specialists, each reading a different slice of the data, each asking a
  different question (What has demand been? Can supply fulfil it? What is the
  financial stake? What market forces are active? What happened last time this
  reason was cited?).

THE RECONCILER
  One Opus call per decision, synthesising all evidence into a plain-English
  recommendation — not a command, a suggestion.

THE MEMORY
  A DuckDB database that accumulates every decision and computes FVA once actuals
  arrive, continuously updating the track record for planners and reason types.

THE UI
  Four numbered steps, plain language throughout, real numbers from real data,
  consequence box that makes the financial stakes visceral, Decision Record that
  becomes the audit trail.

The whole system runs on a laptop, requires no cloud infrastructure beyond an API
key, and the entire state is one DuckDB file plus a directory of parquet files.
That simplicity is a feature — it makes the system inspectable, portable, and fast
to demo.
